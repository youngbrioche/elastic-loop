<PageIntro kicker="Part six" title="Roles">
Agent work needs knowledge from across the team. Product people clarify what is worth building; designers and domain experts help define and assess the result. Engineers make the context, tools, and checks usable. Those responsible for the working process and budget give that work time and ownership. These contributions shape the task from its beginning through review and subsequent use.

## The engineer: designer of the loop

Engineers design and maintain the technical conditions in which agents work. That includes making the application runnable, supplying relevant context, and building quick checks that provide <Term id="backpressure">backpressure</Term>. They also arrange independent reviews and enforce permissions. [Harness](/harness) explains how these mechanisms work together.

For the dashboard, an engineer might encode dependency rules as architecture tests, investigate a reviewer’s security finding, or make a realistic preview available for product feedback. <Term id="harness">Harness</Term> engineering includes improving that setup as the team learns from its use.

## The product owner

Acceptance criteria tell the agent what the result needs to satisfy. Product people contribute examples and explain which rules are settled and which questions still need exploration. A quick, inexpensive check against those criteria can provide backpressure. A more involved assessment belongs at a review gate, performed by a person, an agent, or both.

In practice that splits into seven jobs:

1. Explain the problem, who it affects, and the intended result. Separate firm requirements from assumptions the team can explore.
2. Keep product rules, examples, and decision history available where the agent can find them.
3. Make options and trade-offs visible before committing.
4. Test variants with representative behavior and data, so the team can assess whether they would work in the product.
5. Limit proposed work to what the team can assess and prioritize.
6. Design the evaluation with designers, engineers, testers, and domain experts.
7. Use findings from product use, repairs, and support to guide the next task.

Product people help define the <Term id="search-space">search space</Term> through intent, context, and constraints. They also supply evaluation material, including acceptance scenarios, <Term id="counterexample">counterexamples</Term>, and <Term id="rubric">rubrics</Term>. The team needs these contributions throughout implementation and evaluation.

## The designer

Designers provide the examples and constraints that make work fit the product and its audience. Approved components and brand assets give agents usable context. Fast checks can verify properties such as allowed colors, while a design review assesses the choices those checks leave open. Review findings can lead to better examples or new checks; their effect needs evaluation on subsequent work.

For the dashboard, designers can help the team explore how people understand a priority order and choose what to handle next. The comparison in [Grading](/grading) keeps that product question open while tests verify each proposed rule.

## The domain expert

Domain experts explain the rules, exceptions, and past decisions that an agent may be unable to find in the code or documentation. They can supply <Term id="golden">golden examples</Term> of accepted results and counterexamples that explain why a plausible answer fails. For the dashboard, this might include cases where the apparent urgency hides a contractual or operational consequence. Give experts time to make that knowledge usable.

## The people who run the process

The people responsible for the team’s working process help connect findings across tasks with decisions about improvement. They make room to investigate recurring problems, assign responsibility, and follow up on the effect of changes. The [Factory](/factory) chapter develops this part of the operating model. Harness engineering supplies technical improvements within it.

## The engineering leader

Leaders make the investment and delegation decisions explicit:

- Use evaluations of completed tasks to examine recurring failures, human rework, and evidence of improvement.
- Investigate why a task needs close supervision. Missing context or unreliable checks may be repairable; an unresolved product question may need continued collaboration.
- Budget for maintaining context, checks, and review, including time from product and domain experts.
- Define authority and escalation. The [delegation agreement](/loops#agree-what-the-agent-may-do) distinguishes starting work, reporting progress, and taking actions that need separate approval.

## Do these become new jobs?

Most of these responsibilities can sit within existing roles. A dedicated harness engineering role may make sense when several teams depend on shared tooling and someone needs to own its reliability and development. In a smaller team, an engineer may take that responsibility alongside product work.

Assign responsibility and time for evaluation, access controls, and investment decisions. The [Factory](/factory) chapter describes how the team makes those duties part of its operating model.

## When handoffs stop making sense

Agents can produce drafts, prototypes, and tests quickly enough for several roles to examine the same work together. Product and domain experts can question the behavior while engineers assess the implementation. Some handoffs remain necessary, especially where responsibility or access changes. Others can become a shared review of a working result.
