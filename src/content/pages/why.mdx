<PageIntro kicker="Part two" title="Why">
Why let an agent do more work before you look again? It can give you time to explore alternatives or attempt something you wouldn’t have built yourself. It also leaves you with more work to assess. Whether that trade-off pays off depends on the task, the agent’s working conditions, and how well you can judge the result.
</PageIntro>

## Faster implementation changes where you spend time

When implementation gets faster, the decisions around it can take a larger share of the team’s time:

- At the front: what is worth building, who it is for, what good would look like.
- At the back: whether what came back is any good, and what production teaches you once it ships.

<div class="reading why-intro">

<WhyScene />

When implementation speeds up, review or product decisions can become the limiting step. More work can accumulate while waiting for those decisions. Eliyahu Goldratt’s [Theory of Constraints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_constraints) provides a way to examine such constraints. For your team, how long does work wait for a decision about what to build or whether to accept it?

You may have experienced this: an agent hands you a feature in minutes, and you spend an hour working out whether it does what you wanted. Include that hour when assessing the benefit of delegation.

## Agentic engineering is machine learning

François Chollet describes agentic engineering as a form of machine learning in a [post on X](https://x.com/fchollet/status/2053234697392754701) from May 2026. The engineer defines the goal, constraints, and <Term id="search-space">search space</Term> of possible solutions. The agent generates code and uses evaluation results to revise it. In this view, you assess the result as a <Term id="blackbox-artifact">blackbox artifact</Term>: examine what it does, including on cases beyond those used to develop it. That ability to work on unfamiliar cases is <Term id="generalization">generalization</Term>.

This view puts attention on how the task is framed and evaluated. A specification can state known requirements and leave room to explore unresolved choices. [Grading](/grading) explains how it develops with the work. The <Term id="evaluation-function">evaluation function</Term>, the method used to score a proposed result, needs its own assessment: a passing score is useful only when the criteria reflect what the team needs.

I find the constant focus on productivity boring. I want to know whether we’re building better products and attempting things we previously couldn’t. Counts of pull requests tell us how much work was submitted. We also need to know whether it was useful, what it cost to review, and how much repair it needed.

> What changed because we spent those tokens?

Exploring an unresolved product question may require several candidate solutions. Agree what the experiment should teach you, compare the results, and retain the reasons for your choice. [Factory](/factory) distinguishes that exploration from correcting violations of agreed requirements, drawing on Toyota and <Term id="cynefin">Cynefin</Term>.

Research has examined solution quality alongside speed. In an experiment at Procter & Gamble, [Dell'Acqua, Mollick et al. (2025)](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67197) studied 776 professionals working on product problems. Individuals using AI matched the solution quality of two-person teams without it. The study measured solution quality as well as time spent; the AI groups also completed the work faster. Applying that finding to your work requires a useful way to assess quality.

## Evaluate the result and the criteria

Clear criteria help an agent evaluate and revise its work between human decisions. Tests compare behavior with defined expectations. <Term id="rubric">Rubrics</Term> describe qualities a reviewer should assess, while <Term id="golden">golden</Term> examples show accepted results. An <Term id="llm-judge">LLM judge</Term> or human reviewer can investigate questions the existing checks leave open. Evidence from product use can reveal where the criteria themselves need to change.

Check the criteria against examples people have assessed, including <Term id="counterexample">counterexamples</Term> that look plausible but fail an important requirement. A grader can reward the wrong properties. If it accepts a dashboard because the layout looks polished while urgent cases remain hard to find, the agent may keep improving the layout without addressing that problem.

## Preserve feedback and evaluate its effect

<Term id="retained-feedback">Retained feedback</Term> preserves something useful for later work: a corrected instruction, a test case, or the reasons behind a decision. Record why a variant was rejected and what might justify reconsidering it. To establish that the system improved, examine whether the resulting change helps on later comparable tasks. The [Factory](/factory) chapter describes that evaluation.

## Evaluate the model and its surrounding setup

Comparisons with a fixed model show how much the surrounding setup can affect results. [LangChain reported](https://www.langchain.com/blog/improving-deep-agents-with-harness-engineering) an increase from 52.8% to 66.5% on Terminal-Bench 2.0 while keeping GPT-5.2-Codex fixed. Its <Term id="harness">harness</Term> changes included verification, context delivery, and time management.

Such results give teams a reason to evaluate context and execution mechanisms alongside model choice. To choose an investment, compare the available combinations on tasks that matter to your team, including quality, review effort, and total cost. [Harness](/harness) develops the technical work behind that comparison.

## Keep the work useful and reviewable

Sprawl occurs when generated work exceeds what the team can use or review. <Term id="statistical-middle">Slop</Term> is plausible, generic work that misses the requirements of a particular product. Either can consume the time saved during implementation.

Bound the task, agree who will inspect the result, and give that person enough time and evidence to do so. Before asking for more variants, decide what you need to learn from them.

A generic result may reveal missing product context, weak checks, or a review that rewards the wrong properties. Give the agent the relevant decisions and examples, then use tests and review to find where the result still misses the requirements. Quick, inexpensive feedback supplies <Term id="backpressure">backpressure</Term> during implementation. Review gates assess questions that need more time and judgment. Give reviewers that product context too. [Factory](/factory) shows how to evaluate improvements across comparable tasks.

> Build things that would not exist otherwise.

For your next task, which product decisions and examples would help the agent produce something useful to your users?
